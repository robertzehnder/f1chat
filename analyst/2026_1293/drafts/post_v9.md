# Antonelli wins at Monza from 19th, the first Italian to do it since 1966

Kimi Antonelli won the Italian Grand Prix from 19th on the grid, led a Mercedes one-two and became the first Italian to win at Monza since Ludovico Scarfiotti in 1966. It was his seventh win of the season, and it stretched his championship lead over George Russell to 66 points. He called it a dream coming true. How it was done matters as much as the arithmetic: a tyre split made under the red flag, a virtual safety car that arrived at the right moment, and a chase that took him from fourteen seconds behind his team-mate to a pass on lap 50.

Russell had led for most of the afternoon on a set of hard tyres he hoped would carry him to the flag. Antonelli, on mediums, had the pace but could not build a lasting advantage, and Mercedes did not expect his tyres to reach the finish. When the virtual safety car came on lap 28 the team stopped one car and left the other out, and the race turned on that split.

The grid position was a penalty rather than a qualifying failure. Antonelli had qualified seventh; an engine change, according to the weekend's reporting, cost him 12 places. He gained seven positions in the first two laps. Then Charles Leclerc lost his Ferrari at the final corner on lap 2 and went into the barriers, walking away unhurt, and the race was suspended on lap 3.

The suspension gave every team a free tyre change, and Mercedes used it to split. Russell went from mediums to hards and would try to reach the finish without stopping again. Antonelli went from his starting hards to mediums, which gave him pace and made a second stop more likely rather than certain; Toto Wolff said afterwards that the team did not think that set would go to the end. The restart put Russell first and Antonelli 12th, so the task on the medium car was easy to state and hard to do: reach the front, then get far enough ahead to pay for a stop.

He reached the front sooner than the plan required. Max Verstappen, also on mediums, passed Russell for the lead on lap 12 and held it for three laps before Russell went back past. Antonelli was second by lap 16 and led from lap 18 to lap 22, after which Russell was ahead again. End-of-lap order can hide moves reversed within a lap, so these are the exchanges the timing records rather than every one that happened, but the shape is clear: the two Mercedes swapped the lead and neither built a margin. By lap 27 the gap between them was half a second, with neither driver on fresh rubber.

Lance Stroll's car stopped on lap 27, the cause the reporting gives for the virtual safety car deployed on lap 28. Antonelli came in for a second set of mediums; Russell stayed out. His time in the pit lane was 24.9 seconds, identical to Verstappen's, who stopped a few seconds behind him for hards. Under the VSC that stop cost far less than it would have at racing speed, but it still cost: a gap of half a second at the end of lap 27 became 14.4 seconds by the end of lap 29, and Antonelli rejoined fifth.

Russell said afterwards that he had expected both cars to stay out and was surprised to see his team-mate pit, though he accepted that splitting the cars made sense; his own verdict was that nobody could have known the right call at the time. What the call did was turn a stop Mercedes thought Antonelli would probably need anyway into a cheap one.

The chase took the rest of the race. By the end of lap 40 Antonelli was 7.1 seconds behind; by the end of lap 48 the gap was under three tenths. Across those eight laps it closed at roughly 0.85 seconds a lap. Measured by lap time rather than by gap, his laps from 40 to 48 averaged 0.8 seconds quicker than Russell's. Russell was on hards that were by then more than 40 laps old and, according to Mercedes' trackside engineering director Andrew Shovlin, was suffering vibrations on his front tyres in the closing stages, so his fading pace was a matter of tyre condition rather than age alone.

Then Antonelli nearly threw it away. He ran wide at the first chicane on lap 49, went across the gravel, and race control deleted the lap for track limits. The lap was some four seconds slower than his previous one, but Russell's own lap 49 was slow as well, so the gap grew only to 0.86 seconds. Antonelli said afterwards that when the car went straight on he thought he might have lost his chance.

A lap later he was through, on the run from the second Lesmo down to Ascari midway through lap 50, and this time there was no reply. Once ahead he pulled away at a rate Russell could not match, set the fastest lap of the race on the final tour, a 1m23.504s, and won by 3.857 seconds. Russell had led 40 of the 53 laps.

The virtual safety car did not make Antonelli quick; the mediums did that, and he had already shown he could catch Russell on tyres of the same age. What it did was make his second stop affordable. The stop cost him about 14 seconds of gap under the VSC. The packet's estimate for the same stop at racing speed is about 31 seconds, a figure built from pit-lane time rather than a netted loss, so it is a ceiling rather than a measurement; the true green-flag cost would have been lower, and still well above what he paid.

At the closing rate he found late on, every extra second of deficit would have needed more than a lap to recover, and he passed Russell on lap 50 of 53 with none to spare. Whether he would have won without the VSC is not a question the timing can settle: traffic, the timing of a green-flag stop and the state of both cars' tyres by then all move the answer. What the data supports is narrower and firm enough. The cheaper stop turned pace that had already been demonstrated into a lead that stuck.

For Russell, the frustration is how close it was. His one-stop was the stronger plan for most of the afternoon, and it became vulnerable only when the stop it was designed to avoid became cheap for the other car.

Verstappen stopped under the same VSC, switched to hards and came out seventh; he was third again by lap 38 and, after briefly dropping behind Oscar Piastri, finished there, 14.7 seconds behind the winner. Lando Norris and Piastri raced each other for fourth through the closing laps and swapped the place more than once, wheel to wheel through Curva Grande with Norris briefly on the grass before, by the team's own account, McLaren called the fight off; Norris took fourth by two tenths.

Ferrari's afternoon was defined by lap 2. Leclerc's crash came at the end of a lap that had already put him and Lewis Hamilton side by side at the first chicane, an incident the stewards noted and reviewed without further investigation; the two events were separate, and nothing in the record links them. Hamilton finished sixth. Pierre Gasly had put the Alpine on pole and finished seventh, the first car behind Mercedes, Red Bull, McLaren and Ferrari, a result he called best of the rest; the drop from first to seventh reflects the Alpine's race pace against those four teams rather than any single incident.

Antonelli leaves Monza with 267 points and a 66-point lead over Russell. Madrid is next, on a circuit nobody has raced, and the field arrives there knowing the championship leader can now win from 19th as well as from the front.

## Race notes

Sergio Pérez's five-second penalty was for failing to follow the race director's escape-road instructions at Turn 1 on lap 25; the stewards issued it on lap 30. Yuki Tsunoda's starting-procedure infringement was referred to the stewards for after the race, and the timing feed records no decision. Russell was noted for a yellow-flag infringement on lap 33 and cleared with no further action on lap 40.

## About the data

Timing and race-control data are from OpenF1 for this session, compiled into a race evidence packet that is linked at the foot of this page. Gaps between the two Mercedes are the difference in the time each car crossed the line at the end of a lap, which is why the figures and the text differ slightly from the sampled intervals shown on a broadcast timing screen. The chart's final point is reconstructed from the last lap's start time plus its lap time and differs from the official margin by less than a tenth; the official classification is the margin quoted in the text. Positions are the classified order at the end of each lap; the feed logs changes only, so a position is carried forward until the next logged change. The safety car that preceded the red flag on lap 3 has no recorded end and is shown as ending when the red flag was issued.

The closing rate is quoted two ways: as the change in the gap between the ends of laps 40 and 48, and as the average difference in lap time over laps 40 to 48. They are different quantities. The estimate of what a green-flag stop would have cost is the race's median green pit-lane time and is labelled as an estimate in the packet. The cause of the virtual safety car, the corner of the winning pass, the reason for the grid penalty and all driver and team remarks come from Formula1.com's race reporting and are attributed where they appear; the race-control log itself records only the deployment.
