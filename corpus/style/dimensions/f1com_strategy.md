# Style dimensions — Formula1.com — race strategy guides

> Distilled 2026-09-07 from 7 dev-split documents (see corpus/manifests/distill_style_v1.json). Rules only — no source content.

## STRUCTURE

1. **Open with a bold-italicised scene-setter** (1–3 sentences): establish the current qualifying/pre-race context, name the core strategic tension, and close with a punchy thematic sentence that frames what the rest of the piece will explore.
2. **Anchor the piece in recent precedent** via a dedicated "What happened last year?" (or equivalent) section before any forward-looking analysis; treat prior-year results as the foundational evidence block.
3. **Narrate past strategy chronologically**, moving from race-winner/frontrunners outward to midfield and tail-enders, capturing the full strategic spectrum before moving on.
4. **Pivot to prediction** with a section header phrased as a direct question ("What's the fastest strategy this time?"); this signals the transition from historical record to prescriptive analysis.
5. **Layer secondary questions** (a rest-of-the-field options question; an alternate-plan question for the leading group) to handle alternate strategies, grid-penalty drivers, or lower-order runners in self-contained sub-sections.
6. **Close each sub-section on a concrete recommendation or tension**, never a summary; the final beat of any section introduces either a new variable or an open caveat.

**Skeleton outline:**
```
[BOLD-ITALIC scene-setter: current context → core tension → thematic kicker]

### [Retrospective header: prior race(s)]
[Frontrunner narrative — compound sequence > compound sequence]
[Midfield / outlier narrative]
[Tail / unusual strategy note]

### [Forward-looking question header]
[Simulation/data anchor]
[Primary recommendation: compound>compound(>compound), pit-window range]
[Complicating variable or caveat]

### [Secondary question header — optional, repeatable]
[Alternate strategy with windows]
[Specific team/driver application]
[Closing tension or open question]
```

## EVIDENTIARY PRACTICE

1. **Simulation data is cited as institutional voice**, attributed to a named tyre-supplier simulation or a named technical authority, always paraphrased and introduced with a reporting verb ("the sims suggest," "according to sims").
2. **Pit-lap windows are always expressed as ranges** ("Laps X–Y"), never as single-point predictions; this signals probabilistic, not deterministic, confidence.
3. **Compound sequences serve as shorthand evidence** — the compound-arrow notation (medium>hard>soft) functions as a compact empirical record, doing the work of a table.
4. **Past-race outcomes are treated as the primary data set**; season-level trends are cited as a secondary, potentially conflicting layer, and the tension between them is named explicitly rather than resolved silently.
5. **Degradation figures and lap-time deltas are quoted sparingly** (one or two per piece) and only when they explain a strategic implication, never as decoration.
6. **Speculation is hedged with modality markers** ("may," "could," "looks like," "realistically") and contrasted immediately with a firmer assertion to maintain credibility balance.
7. **Qualitative characterisations of tyre behaviour** ("brutal," "virtually no degradation," "not behaving as expected") are used as evidence bridges between raw data and strategic conclusion.

---

## REGISTER

1. **Authoritative-but-conversational**: the writing assumes the reader is competent and interested, not a beginner; jargon (undercut, tyre delta, VSC, DRS) is used without definition.
2. **Second person is avoided**; the reader is addressed indirectly through impersonal constructions or collective "we" that positions writer and reader as co-analysts.
3. **Contractions and ellipsis** (em-dash asides, sentence fragments, trailing "…") are used freely to create a spoken, live-briefing feel within otherwise structured prose.
4. **Superlatives and emphatic doublings** ("very, very," "really, really") are permitted for rhetorical emphasis at moments of strategic significance, mimicking a pit-wall engineer's verbal stress.
5. **Irony and dry understatement** are used for comedy beats at the expense of poor strategic outcomes or unusual tyre choices, but never at the expense of analytical rigour.
6. **Technical compound nomenclature** (C1, C3, soft, medium, hard, intermediate) is treated as proper vocabulary and mixed freely with colloquial synonyms ("the white-banded compound," "their medicine").
7. **Driver and team names are treated as common knowledge**; no credentials, nationalities, or backstory are supplied unless they are directly strategically relevant.

---

## PACING

1. **Opening bold-italic paragraph is dense and short** (2–4 sentences maximum); it functions as a headline, not an introduction, and must earn its pace with information density.
2. **Historical narrative paragraphs are medium-length** (3–6 sentences), each covering one strategic thread; a new paragraph signals a new competitor or strategic variant, not a new idea within the same thread.
3. **The pivotal prediction section is the piece's density peak**: compound sequences, pit windows, degradation rates, and simulation attribution are stacked closely, with minimal discursive padding.
4. **Secondary option sections are shorter than the primary recommendation**, telegraphing their subordinate status through brevity alone.
5. **Single-sentence paragraphs are used strategically** at moments of reversal, surprise, or strong assertion — they act as editorial full-stops after complex multi-clause buildup.
6. **The piece never lingers on a single point past its natural close**; once a strategy option is characterised and windowed, the prose moves immediately to the next variable or complication.
7. **Expert quotations, when present, interrupt the analytical flow briefly** then are immediately absorbed back into the writer's own analytical voice within the same or next paragraph.
