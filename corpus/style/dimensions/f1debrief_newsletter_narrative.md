# Style dimensions — F1 Debrief — per-race newsletter narrative

> Distilled 2026-09-08 from 6 dev-split documents (see corpus/manifests/distill_style_v1.json). Rules only — no source content.

## STRUCTURE

**Rules:**

1. **Lead with a punchy, self-contained hook** — one or two short sentences that frame the emotional or competitive stakes of the piece before any scene-setting begins; often an unexpected superlative, comparison, or ironic observation.
2. **Establish the dominant narrative thread immediately**, then branch into secondary stories as clearly labelled sub-sections with bold headers, each opening with its own mini-hook.
3. **Build chronologically within each thread** — recreate the sequence of decisions, incidents, and consequences in the order they unfolded, anchoring the reader's sense of cause and effect before offering interpretation.
4. **Let driver or team-principal quotes serve as structural pivots**: a quote typically closes one argumentative beat and opens the next, rather than appearing as isolated decoration.
5. **Close the main thread with a forward-looking sentence or rhetorical question** that ties the race's outcome to the championship picture; sub-sections close with a single evaluative sentence rather than a summary paragraph.

**Skeleton outline:**

```
[HOOK — 1–2 sentences: stakes, irony, or superlative]

[SCENE — 2–3 sentences: situation entering the key moment]

[SEQUENCE OF EVENTS — 3–5 beats, each 1–3 paragraphs:
  Beat 1: inciting incident + immediate consequence
  Beat 2: complicating factor or strategic decision
  Beat 3: turning point
  Beat 4: resolution
  Beat 5 (optional): residual effect on standings]

[PIVOT QUOTE — driver or principal voice closes the thread]

[FORWARD-LOOKING CLOSE — 1–2 sentences: championship implications or open question]

---

## [SUB-SECTION HEADER — secondary storyline]

[MINI-HOOK — 1 sentence]
[INCIDENT EXPLANATION — technical or regulatory detail, 2–4 paragraphs]
[INSTITUTIONAL RESPONSE — team/FIA reaction]
[EVALUATIVE CLOSE — 1 sentence verdict]
```

## EVIDENTIARY PRACTICE

**Rules:**

1. **Technical claims are grounded in process description**, not assertion alone — when a regulation, system, or procedure is invoked, the writer briefly explains the mechanism (how the measurement works, what the equipment does, what the rulebook says) before drawing a conclusion.
2. **Precise figures are used sparingly and purposefully**: speed differentials, time gaps, penalty amounts, and championship points gaps appear when they are the most efficient way to convey magnitude; they are never clustered decoratively.
3. **Speculation is flagged with explicit hedges** ("it is likely," "seems to suggest," "whether that also translates… remains to be seen") and is confined to forward-looking statements; past events are stated as fact or attributed.
4. **Driver and team-principal quotes are treated as primary evidence**, given equal or greater weight than the writer's own inference; quotes are introduced with minimal framing and allowed to speak at length.
5. **Causation is distinguished from correlation**: when luck or timing influences an outcome, the writer names it explicitly rather than folding it silently into the result, often using phrases that acknowledge contingency ("a fortuitous bit of timing," "one man's misfortune").
6. **Regulatory and technical detail is cited by function rather than memorised verbatim** — the reader is told what a rule does and why it matters in this specific situation, not given a comprehensive lecture.

## REGISTER

**Rules:**

1. **Conversational authority**: the writer addresses the reader as an intelligent enthusiast, not a novice — technical vocabulary (harvesting limits, deployment, undercut, superclip) is used without definition unless the term is obscure or newly relevant to the season.
2. **Colloquial asides and dry wit** are deployed at roughly one-per-section frequency to prevent the technical passages from becoming arid ("You read that right"; a sporting comparison that lands as a punchline); they are brief and never sarcastic toward drivers.
3. **First-person is absent from most of the text** but appears in isolated, confident evaluative moments ("I have a feeling we'll be saying that more often") — used as punctuation of conviction, not as ongoing narrator presence.
4. **Emotional register tracks the drama**: language escalates in specificity and energy during pivotal moments ("emerged a car-length ahead," "furious, in a radio message") and relaxes into measured analysis between them.
5. **Fairness as a stylistic principle**: critical assessment of teams, drivers, and officials is delivered without mockery; even when a team makes a poor decision or a driver is penalised, the framing remains analytical rather than contemptuous.
6. **Sub-clause-heavy sentences are reserved for technical exposition**; narrative action is delivered in short, punchy constructions.

---

## PACING

**Rules:**

1. **The opening section moves fastest**: the hook-and-scene establishment rarely exceeds three short paragraphs before the first quote or incident lands, resisting the urge to contextualise before dramatising.
2. **Density peaks at the turning point of the main narrative**: the paragraph describing the decisive moment (overtake, crash, penalty, strategic call) is the most detail-rich in the piece — more precise figures, more quoted radio, more step-by-step sequencing — then releases into shorter, reflective sentences.
3. **One-sentence paragraphs are used as deliberate punctuation marks** — to end a beat cleanly, to introduce irony, or to land an evaluative verdict; they appear roughly every 300–400 words.
4. **Quotes break up long explanatory passages**: no more than three consecutive paragraphs of authorial prose appear without a driver or team voice interjecting; this creates a natural call-and-response rhythm.
5. **Sub-sections are shorter than the main thread** — typically three to five paragraphs — and move at a brisker pace, since their function is to complete the picture rather than carry the emotional weight.
6. **The piece ends quickly**: the closing thought of both the main thread and the final sub-section is delivered in one or two sentences, with no summarising coda; the reader is left with a question or a forward implication, not a recap.
