# Style dimensions — Mark Hughes — flowing analytical race narrative (The Race, 2025)

> Distilled 2026-09-08 from 22 dev-split documents (see corpus/manifests/distill_style_v1.json). Rules only — no source content.

## STRUCTURE

**Rules:**

1. Open with a sharp declarative statement or question that frames the entire analytical thesis — not scene-setting, but immediate positioning of the competitive argument.
2. Follow the opening with a qualifying subordinate clause that complicates or nuances the lead claim, preventing any clean reading of the situation.
3. Build the body through a series of nested analytical layers: establish headline observation → supply contextual correction → introduce mechanical explanation → extend to strategic consequence.
4. Embed ranked tabular data (long-run averages with run lengths and compound noted) mid-piece as a structural anchor, not an appendix — the table interrupts the prose deliberately and is immediately interrogated.
5. Place driver quotations as confirmation or complication of claims already established analytically — quotes never lead, they respond.
6. Introduce secondary-team analysis only after the lead battle is resolved, in descending competitive relevance.
7. Close with a forward-looking sentence of moderate certainty, signalling what the weekend's open variables will decide — never a summary, always a lean into the next session.

**Skeleton outline:**

```
[DECLARATIVE THESIS SENTENCE — competitive claim or framing question]

[COMPLICATING SUBORDINATE — run length / tyre compound / session context correction]

[MECHANICAL EXPLANATION LAYER — why the numbers mean what they mean]

[TABULAR DATA BLOCK]
  Driver | Average | Laps | Compound

[CONTEXTUAL INTERROGATION OF TABLE — which entries to discount and why]

[SECONDARY TEAM BLOCK — one to three teams in order, each in a short paragraph]

[STRATEGIC/NARRATIVE BRIDGE — what the data implies for the next session]

[CLOSING FORWARD LEAN — conditional prediction, no certainty asserted]
```

## EVIDENTIARY PRACTICE

**Rules:**

1. Raw session times are never accepted as face value — every number is immediately adjusted for run length, fuel load, tyre compound, traffic, or thermal state before any competitive conclusion is drawn.
2. Run length is treated as a primary confounding variable: longer stints depress average pace, so the author routinely normalises or flags the disparity before comparing cars.
3. Sector-level GPS or speed-trap data is cited as the granular evidence beneath the lap time; gains and losses are localized to specific corner sequences, not generalised.
4. Speculation is hedged through conditional phrasing ("would not be a surprise," "may have been," "probably") but mechanical claims — tyre physics, aero behaviour — are stated declaratively once the author judges them established.
5. Tyre compound and state (new vs. used, laps into a set) are tracked and declared whenever they might alter a competitive reading; the absence of a like-for-like comparison is explicitly noted rather than papered over.
6. Team principal and engineer quotations are used as primary evidence of internal car state (balance, tyre feel, mode level) rather than as colour — the analytical point is extracted from the quote, not the social content.
7. Historical or cross-weekend precedent is invoked quantitatively where possible (comparative qualifying percentages, year-on-year lap time deltas) to anchor current observations in a broader evidence frame.

## REGISTER

**Rules:**

1. Vocabulary is technical but not jargon-heavy; compound technical terms (tyre thermal state, aero map, ride compliance, bulk versus surface temperature) are used precisely and without definition — the assumed reader is knowledgeable but not an engineer.
2. The author's analytical voice is confident and first-person-absent — opinions are stated as findings, not prefaced with "I think."
3. Rhetorical questions are used sparingly and only to mark a genuine pivot in the argument (shifting from one team to another, or from qualifying to race analysis) — never as decoration.
4. Occasional dry wit is embedded as a short parenthetical or sentence fragment, never developed into a joke; understatement is preferred over emphasis.
5. The reader is not directly addressed; the piece is written as if thinking aloud at the highest level of the subject, with the audience trusted to follow without hand-holding.
6. Qualifying caveats are embedded mid-sentence rather than isolated — the author does not stop to say "this is uncertain"; uncertainty is grammatically folded into the claim itself.
7. Precision in physical description (distances in metres, speeds in km/h or mph, time deltas to thousandths) signals epistemic authority — vague language is reserved for genuinely unknown quantities.

## PACING

**Rules:**

1. The opening paragraph is short (one to three sentences) and dense — maximum information, minimum framing.
2. Mechanical explanation paragraphs are the longest in the piece, running four to eight sentences, as analytical depth is dwelt upon rather than summarised.
3. After any extended mechanical or strategic section, the prose resets with a very short sentence (one clause) as a breath-point before the next analytical layer begins.
4. Tabular data creates a mandatory hard stop in rhythm; the passage immediately before the table accelerates in sentence length (shorter, declarative), the passage immediately after begins with a caution or qualification to slow the reader back down.
5. Driver quote paragraphs are brief (the quote plus one to two sentences of extraction); they do not expand into additional commentary — they close a point rather than open one.
6. The midfield section at the end of a piece is handled at pace — each team or driver gets a sentence or two, never a paragraph — creating a deliberate gear-change downward before the closing line.
7. The final sentence is syntactically open-ended (conditional clause, forward-looking modal verb) and short — it should feel unresolved, creating anticipatory tension rather than closure.
