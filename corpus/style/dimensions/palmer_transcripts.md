# Style dimensions — Jolyon Palmer — onboard/telemetry video analysis (spoken)

> Distilled 2026-09-07 from 7 dev-split documents (see corpus/manifests/distill_style_v1.json). Rules only — no source content.

## STRUCTURE

1. **Hook via immediate action**: Open mid-scene, dropping the audience directly into a specific moment ("So the start of the race, and…"), with zero preamble or scene-setting.
2. **Thesis embedded in first observation**: State the central analytical claim within the first two sentences, framed as a phenomenon to be explained rather than a question to be answered.
3. **Accordion zoom**: Alternate between wide-field (all frontrunners, strategic picture) and tight-focus (one driver, one data trace) passes through the same material, each pass adding a layer of reasoning.
4. **Pivot marker sentences**: Signal each new sub-topic with a short declarative bridge ("But it wasn't the only…"; "Fast forward…"; "So let's have a look at…") that resets the reader's attention without transitional formality.
5. **Counter-argument absorption**: Briefly voice the opposing interpretation, then rebut or qualify it before moving on — never ignore it.
6. **Hypothetical closing arm**: End one or more major sections with a counterfactual ("What if…"), answered with data, before returning to what actually happened.
7. **Emotional/human coda**: Close the overall piece with a brief commentary on driver emotion, rivalry stakes, or a sporting judgment, shifting register from analytical to reflective.

**Skeleton outline:**
```
[OPEN] Immediate scene drop + central phenomenon stated
[WIDE PASS] Field-level observation — who gained/lost, why it matters
[TIGHT PASS 1] Single actor, close camera/data — mechanism explained
[COUNTER / QUALIFICATION] Alternative reading acknowledged and addressed
[TIGHT PASS 2] Second actor or comparative trace — contrast established
[HYPOTHETICAL ARM] "What if X?" — answered with evidence
[WIDER IMPLICATION] Championship / strategic consequence named
[HUMAN CODA] Sporting or emotional judgment; brief driver-voice quote
```

## EVIDENTIARY PRACTICE

1. **Telemetry as primary witness**: Data traces are treated as the highest-grade evidence; verbal claims are validated or overturned by reference to a specific line on a graph, a throttle percentage, or a speed delta.
2. **Graduated confidence language**: Assertions scale explicitly from "you can clearly see" (high certainty) → "it looks to me like" (inference) → "maybe," "possibly," "I wonder if" (speculation) — and these gradations are never collapsed.
3. **Quantified comparisons over raw numbers**: Numbers almost always appear as differences or ratios within a sentence (a per-lap delta expressed as a spoken range; a fraction-of-a-tenth comparison) rather than as isolated statistics.
4. **Historical precedent as illustrative parallel**: A past event or earlier moment in the same race is introduced not as trivia but as structural evidence — "it's a similar position to…" — to show a pattern is real, not coincidental.
5. **Negative evidence used**: What a driver did *not* do, or what the data does *not* show, is cited as evidence just as often as positive observations ("he's not actually managing to reclaim any speed…").
6. **Steward/regulation standard as falsifiable benchmark**: Regulatory criteria (e.g., what a driver must achieve to satisfy the racing guidelines) are stated explicitly so the viewer can apply them to the footage and reach their own verdict.
7. **Simulation and counterfactual flagged as such**: Projected outcomes are explicitly labeled ("according to our simulations"; "what if no…?") and distinguished from observed fact.
8. **Self-correction in real time**: When an initial reading proves incomplete on closer inspection, the revision is stated openly and attributed to the new evidence rather than quietly dropped.

## REGISTER

1. **Expert-conversational hybrid**: Vocabulary sits at the level of an intelligent sports fan who follows technical detail — technical terms are used without apology but immediately grounded in observable effect ("MGU-K," followed instantly by what that means for the car's behavior).
2. **First-person judgment, openly owned**: Opinions are marked as personal ("I think"; "for me"; "I disagree") rather than presented as consensus, and the speaker does not hedge into false neutrality on questions where evidence permits a view.
3. **Colloquial punctuation of dense passages**: After a sequence of technical analysis, a short vernacular phrase releases tension ("it's pretty insane"; "all looks pretty hunky-dory"; "that takes the mickey").
4. **Driver empathy as analytical tool**: The analyst explicitly inhabits the driver's cognitive state ("if I was George Russell"; "you're looking at the lights, your heart is pounding") to explain decisions, treating psychology as a legitimate evidentiary register.
5. **Direct address used sparingly but precisely**: "You" is used to place the viewer in the driver's seat or in the engineer's position, not as filler — it marks moments of imaginative transfer.
6. **Institutional irreverence**: Governing-body decisions, team principals' statements, and official penalties are evaluated on their merits and criticized when the analyst judges them wrong — authority is not deferred to by default.
7. **Enthusiasm as signal, not noise**: Genuine excitement ("such fine margins"; "absolutely terrific fighting") appears at moments of genuine analytical surprise, not as padding, so it retains meaning.

## PACING

1. **Short sentence for the decisive moment**: When the key event occurs, sentence length drops sharply — sometimes to a fragment — to mirror the instantaneous nature of the action.
2. **Long sentence for mechanism**: Causal chains (why something happened, how a sequence unfolded) are delivered in extended, clause-laden sentences that mirror the accumulation of contributing factors.
3. **Single-point dwell governed by evidence depth**: The piece stays on one moment as long as the data or camera angle yields new information; it moves on the moment the evidence is exhausted, not after a fixed word count.
4. **Replay as pacing device**: Returning to the same clip from a new angle or with new data resets the reader's clock, allowing density to peak multiple times across the piece rather than building to a single climax.
5. **Density peaks at technical cruxes**: The most clause-dense, data-rich paragraphs cluster around the moment that most directly answers the central question; surrounding passages are lighter to make these peaks register.
6. **Rhythm varied by speaker role**: Pure analytical passages use declarative rhythm (assertion → evidence → implication); passages of sporting judgment shift to shorter, more staccato phrasing that signals the writer is now editorializing rather than reporting.
7. **Quote as hard stop**: Driver or team-radio quotes function as full stops on a section — they are placed at the end of an analytical passage to confirm or complicate the reading just given, not to introduce new threads.
