# Style dimensions — Edd Straw — per-race driver ratings

> Distilled 2026-09-07 from 8 dev-split documents (see corpus/manifests/distill_style_v1.json). Rules only — no source content.

## STRUCTURE

1. **Open with a one- or two-sentence scene-setter** that names a defining characteristic of the weekend and telegraphs that the rankings will reflect it — optionally noting the rankings diverge from raw results.
2. **Follow immediately with a teaser hook** (one sentence) pointing toward surprising top or bottom placings without naming drivers.
3. **Insert a fixed-text methodology block** verbatim before the first entry; it explains the ranking philosophy, the "qualifying as lap 0" principle, the car-potential baseline, and the week-to-week volatility caveat.
4. **Structure each driver entry as a named module** containing: [STARTED / FINISHED grid positions] → [PROS bullet list] → [CONS bullet list] → [single-sentence Verdict].
5. **Order entries strictly best-to-worst**, letting position carry the argumentative weight; no explicit inter-driver comparisons outside individual entries.
6. **Close each entry at the Verdict line** — no coda, no section summary, no overall wrap-up paragraph.
7. **Skeleton outline:**
   - `[Weekend characterisation sentence] + [teaser hook sentence]`
   - `[Methodology boilerplate]`
   - For each of N drivers: `[Photo] → [Grid/Finish] → PROS (2–5 bullets) → CONS (1–4 bullets) → Verdict (1 sentence)`
8. Optionally bracket the methodology block with community-engagement callouts (membership sign-up links) that sit outside the ranking logic entirely.
9. In sprint-weekend editions, the prose-paragraph format may replace the bullet structure, but PROS/CONS logic is preserved implicitly.
10. Tier labels ("second tier," "final tier") may be inserted mid-list to group clusters of similarly-performing drivers, but sparingly and only when a clear performance boundary exists.

## EVIDENTIARY PRACTICE

1. **Quantify with precision whenever possible**: lap-time gaps are stated to three decimal places; positional swings and stint lengths are given as exact integers — numbers anchor judgements rather than decorating them.
2. **The primary benchmark is always the team-mate**: every driver's performance is first assessed against their intra-team counterpart before external comparisons are introduced.
3. **Car-potential ceiling is treated as a hard constraint**: a driver finishing where their car's pace dictates is neither praised nor blamed; praise is reserved for beating that ceiling, criticism for falling short of it.
4. **Mitigating factors are acknowledged explicitly before they are weighed**: equipment failure, lost practice time, external impairment, and out-of-control incidents are named and then discounted from the negative ledger only to the degree they genuinely explain the deficit.
5. **Driver self-quotation functions as corroborating testimony**: brief quotes (attributed in paraphrase or partial quotation) are used to confirm that a reported error or struggle is accepted by the driver themselves, lending authority to the verdict.
6. **Speculation is hedged with modal verbs** ("could have," "might have," "possibly") and probability language ("likely," "every chance," "perhaps") — unhedged assertions are reserved for outcomes that actually occurred.
7. **Fortune/luck is treated as a named variable**: when a result is partly explained by events outside driver control, this is labelled explicitly and weighed against, not added to, driver credit.
8. **Comparative pace claims reference qualifying session and stint data**, not just race positions; underlying pace is distinguished from result when safety cars, strategy, or traffic intervene.
9. **Absence of evidence is itself evidence**: when a team-mate comparison is rendered inconclusive by car problems, this is stated directly rather than omitted.
10. **Small negatives are never suppressed**: a pitlane speeding violation by a fraction of a km/h, a single lock-up, or a track-limits violation is listed in CONS even when clearly immaterial to the final result.

## REGISTER

1. **Authoritative but conversational**: the voice is that of an informed expert speaking directly to a knowledgeable audience, not a neutral reporter or a cheerleader — opinions are stated as conclusions, not floated as possibilities.
2. **Technicality is domain-specific, not jargon-dense**: terms like "deployment," "parc ferme," "undercut," "ERS," "VSC" are used without definition, assuming reader fluency; mechanical and aerodynamic phenomena are named precisely.
3. **Personality surfaces through selective emphasis**: a particularly impressive lap earns an emphatic adjective ("superb," "outstanding," "razor-sharp"); a particularly bad error earns a blunt noun ("blunder," "mess," "nonsense").
4. **The reader is addressed as a peer**, never lectured; the methodology block is the only didactic passage and it appears only once per piece.
5. **Verdicts are epigrammatic**: the closing sentence of each entry is compressed, often structured as [quality observed] + [qualifier or counterpoint] — brevity signals finality.
6. **Irony and understatement are used sparingly** but intentionally: a driver finishing last in a ranking described as "as strong a 22nd as you'll see" signals that external circumstances, not ability, are the cause.
7. **Context from earlier in the season** is invoked briefly and without extensive backstory — a single clause suffices (e.g. a clause noting a prior tough weekend, or a first-since-[timeframe] marker).
8. **Sympathy is extended, not sentimentality**: when a driver is poorly ranked through no fault of their own, this is noted factually rather than apologetically.
9. **Hyperbole is almost entirely absent**: strong performances are praised through precise description of difficulty overcome rather than superlatives.
10. **Active voice dominates**: passive constructions appear only when the agent is genuinely unknown or irrelevant (e.g., "was hit by," "was eliminated after").

## PACING

1. **Each driver entry is self-contained and approximately equal in word count** at the default tier (roughly 60–120 words per entry), creating a scanning-friendly rhythm across the full piece.
2. **Bullet points enforce compression**: each PRO or CON is a single clause or sentence — elaboration beyond one sentence signals exceptional importance.
3. **Density peaks in the Verdict line**: the single sentence must absorb the net judgement; any compression failure here undermines the entire entry.
4. **Prose-format entries (Canadian GP style) are reserved for weekend contexts** with sprint races or exceptional complexity; they run longer (150–250 words) but maintain a single-paragraph-per-driver constraint.
5. **The top-ranked driver's entry is typically the longest**, earning elaboration as justification for the top ranking; the bottom-ranked driver's entry may be nearly as long if the case is contentious.
6. **Middle-tier entries move fastest**: drivers finishing near their car's expected position with no significant errors or errors receive the shortest entries, often with minimalist CONS ("Nothing more than a couple of minor violations").
7. **A change in entry length signals a change in analytical weight**: a sudden long entry mid-list flags that something unexpectedly interesting happened to that driver.
8. **No transitional paragraphs between entries**: the photo/grid-position header carries all the transition needed; prose continuity is sacrificed in favour of modular scanability.
9. **Tier-label sentences, when used, are short and declarative** — one sentence only, inserted as a paragraph break.
10. **The methodology block is the only section that never varies in length or tone**: it functions as a tempo-neutral reset before the ranked content begins.
