# Style dimensions — The Race — post-race analysis features

> Distilled 2026-09-07 from 31 dev-split documents (see corpus/manifests/distill_style_v1.json). Rules only — no source content.

## STRUCTURE

1. **Open with a reorienting frame.** Begin each piece (or entry) by situating the reader in context — the track's peculiarities, the season narrative at stake, or the result's surface paradox — before introducing any individual verdict. The frame is typically 1–3 short paragraphs.

2. **Use a binary, explicitly labelled architecture.** Organise the body around winner/loser or learn/reveal headings. Each labelled section stands alone; readers should be able to enter at any point without losing orientation.

3. **Lead each section with the sharpest claim, not the scene-setting.** The first sentence of every section is evaluative, not descriptive. Evidence follows the verdict, not the reverse.

4. **Build each entry in three acts:** (a) the outcome in plain terms, (b) the mechanism that caused it, (c) a qualifying coda — residual doubt, future consequence, or irony that complicates the verdict.

5. **Close entries, not the overall piece.** Individual bylines end each section; the piece itself ends when the last entry ends. No wrap-up paragraph, no final synthesis. The cumulative weight of the entries *is* the conclusion.

6. **Skeleton outline for a post-event winners/losers feature:**
   - [1–3 sentence reorienting frame: season stakes + this event's peculiarity]
   - `## Winner/Loser: [Subject] ([finishing position or outcome tag])`
   - [Verdict sentence] → [mechanism: 2–4 sentences] → [coda: 1–2 sentences] — *Byline*
   - [Repeat section block × N, alternating or grouping winners/losers as narrative logic demands]

7. **Skeleton outline for an "everything we learned" feature:**
   - [1–2 sentence scene-setter naming the dominant theme]
   - `## [Thematic heading in declarative or noun-phrase form]`
   - [Expanded treatment: 3–6 paragraphs, including at least one direct quotation block]
   - [Repeat × N with no fixed order; sequence follows narrative weight]

## EVIDENTIARY PRACTICE

1. **Lap-delta and gap figures anchor claims about pace.** Specific numerical margins (gaps in seconds, tenths, or percentage of pace) are cited as primary evidence; they are not rounded or vague. Numbers are used surgically — one precise figure per claim, not clusters.

2. **Driver radio and post-session quotation serves as confession or corroboration.** Quotes are selected because the subject either condemns themselves, confirms the analyst's reading, or reveals something the timesheets do not show. Quotes are introduced with minimal preamble.

3. **Sector- and corner-level telemetry is cited to explain 'how', not just 'what'.** Claims about pace advantage are routinely traced to a specific corner, braking zone, or straight — often with reference to named turns by number.

4. **Speculation is introduced with explicit epistemic hedges:** "probably," "appears to," "it seems," "likely." Assertions of certainty are reserved for outcomes that are fully documented (finishing positions, penalties, retirements).

5. **Counterfactuals are used to establish magnitude.** To show how significant a result is, the piece frequently poses what *would* have happened without a specific incident — but frames it conditionally, not as fact.

6. **Team-internal comparisons (team-mate gaps, intra-team qualifying head-to-heads) are the default competitive benchmark**, preferred over absolute championship position as evidence of individual performance quality.

7. **Context tables and comparative lists appear when the midfield is the subject.** A formatted standings excerpt or session-order list is embedded directly in the text to anchor claims about inter-team hierarchy without lengthy prose description.

## REGISTER

1. **Vocabulary is sophisticated but not specialist-exclusive.** Technical terms (deployment, aero load, undercut, degradation) are used without definition, assuming a knowledgeable enthusiast audience. Jargon is precise, not decorative.

2. **Personality is present but controlled.** Writers use first-person sparingly — to flag a judgment call, to acknowledge a contrarian take, or to express genuine surprise. Personal voice is an accent, not the whole meal.

3. **Irony and dry wit are preferred over exclamation.** Comic effect arrives through juxtaposition, understatement, or the deliberately flat recounting of an absurd situation. Exclamation marks are rare and deliberate.

4. **The reader is addressed as a peer who has already watched the session.** No race recap is provided. The writer assumes the reader knows what happened; the piece's job is to interpret, not narrate.

5. **Driver and team names are used without honorifics or introduction.** Subjects are named on first reference as if already known; full names appear at the start of each section header but subsequent references use surname only.

6. **Hedging and directness coexist in the same paragraph.** A writer can assert a verdict forcefully and then immediately qualify it — this tension is a stylistic signature, not inconsistency.

7. **Colloquial phrases appear at moments of peak evaluation** — to deliver a verdict that dry analysis alone cannot. These are isolated and contextually motivated, not scattered.

## PACING

1. **Paragraph length is short: typically 2–4 sentences.** A paragraph rarely exceeds five sentences. Ideas are compartmentalised; each paragraph makes one point and exits.

2. **Sentence length varies within each paragraph.** A long analytical sentence is usually followed by a short punchy one that delivers the verdict or the irony. This creates a push-pull rhythm throughout.

3. **Each winner/loser section runs 80–250 words.** Sections rarely exceed this; the discipline of byline attribution creates a natural ceiling. Density peaks in the mechanical-explanation act, not the opening verdict.

4. **"Everything we learned" sections run longer (200–500 words) and accommodate extended quotation.** Pacing slows to allow driver or team representative speech to breathe; the analyst steps back during the quotation block and returns for a closing interpretive sentence.

5. **The piece does not dwell equally on all subjects.** More important or more complex stories get longer sections; clear-cut results get two or three sentences and a byline. Length signals editorial judgment about significance.

6. **Transition between sections is abrupt.** No bridging sentence links one entry to the next. The heading does the transitional work. This keeps pace high and prevents the piece from feeling like a continuous essay.

7. **Density peaks early in each section, then relaxes into the coda.** The first two sentences carry maximum analytical load; the final sentence broadens or complicates, often gesturing toward the next race or a longer arc — a rhythm that gives each section a sense of forward momentum even as it concludes.
