# Style dimensions — The Race — post-race features (secondary register)

> Distilled 2026-09-08 from 67 dev-split documents (see corpus/manifests/distill_style_v1.json). Rules only — no source content.

## STRUCTURE

1. **Open with a contextualizing hook**, either a single declarative sentence establishing stakes, a brief scene-setting observation (1-2 sentences), or a teaser list of 3-4 storylines — then close with a transition line ("Here's our pick of…", "Here's what we learned…") that signals the format to follow.

2. **Use a binary "Winner / Loser" frame (or equivalent "star / flop" framing) as the primary organizational spine**, with each entry titled by the driver or team name and their finishing position or categorical label; "Everything we learned" variants replace binary headers with thematic subheadings but keep the same episodic cadence.

3. **Each entry follows a micro-essay skeleton**: (a) *Orienting claim* — one sentence that delivers the verdict immediately; (b) *Evidential elaboration* — 2-5 sentences of supporting detail, often including a telling quote or stat; (c) *Complicating nuance or forward implication* — 1-2 sentences that acknowledge caveats, wider context, or what comes next; (d) *Optional closing kicker* — a short, punchy sentence that re-anchors the verdict.

4. **Signal authorship at entry level** (byline initials appended after each entry), enabling multi-author pieces with consistent voice while allowing attribution variety.

5. **Sequence entries by dramatic or argumentative logic**, not finishing order: the most significant or surprising result tends to lead; within winners/losers columns, contrast is built by alternating emotional registers (e.g., triumph → heartbreak → underdog win).

6. **Skeleton outline for a "Winners and Losers" piece**:
   - [1–3 sentence hook / tease]
   - [Transition line]
   - **[WINNER / LOSER]: [Name] ([result/label])**
   - [Orienting claim sentence]
   - [2–5 sentences of evidence and texture]
   - [1–2 sentences of nuance or implication]
   - [Optional kicker] *— [Author initials]*
   - *[Repeat for 6–14 entries]*

7. **Skeleton outline for an "Everything we learned" piece**:
   - [1–2 sentence framing line]
   - [Transition line]
   - **[Thematic subheading as a declarative claim]**
   - [4–8 sentences unpacking that claim with evidence, quotes, and context]
   - *[Author initials]*
   - *[Repeat for 6–10 themes]*

## EVIDENTIARY PRACTICE

1. **Treat the qualifying result, race position, or championship implication as the prima facie fact**; all analysis builds outward from that anchor.

2. **Deploy lap-time deltas, points gaps, and positional changes numerically** but integrate them into prose rather than bullet lists — numbers are used to calibrate magnitude ("half a second," "a tenth away," "four places higher") rather than to summarize comprehensively.

3. **Ground interpretive claims in driver or team radio / post-session quotes**, cited briefly and selectively; quotes do double duty as evidence *and* characterization.

4. **Distinguish clearly between what the data shows and what it might mean**: hedging language ("appears to," "suggests," "on balance," "probably") is reserved for inference; declarative language is used when the result speaks for itself.

5. **Acknowledge mitigation or countervailing factors before dismissing them** ("for what it's worth," "to be fair," "with the caveat that"), then reassert the primary verdict — the balance of evidence still points one way.

6. **Use comparative benchmarking as a primary evidential move**: a driver's result is weighed against their team-mate, against the same driver at the previous race, or against historical precedent at that circuit.

7. **Speculation about causes of performance differences is flagged as open questions** ("whether that was … or … is unclear," "it's hard to know") rather than presented as settled fact; mechanical explanations are given conditional rather than definitive framing.

8. **Quote race engineer or team-boss language sparingly but pointedly** — a single well-chosen team-boss quote often closes an evidentiary chain more efficiently than additional author analysis.

## REGISTER

1. **Default to a confident, lightly combative analytical voice** — opinions are stated as verdicts rather than hedged as possibilities; sentences are declarative and the author rarely equivocates about their own judgment.

2. **Calibrate technicality to context**: mechanical and aerodynamic concepts are named precisely (undercut, DRS, floor load, deployment) but not defined; the implied reader is an engaged F1 fan, not an engineer.

3. **Allow controlled colloquialism and wit** — dry irony, self-aware hyperbole, and the occasional rhetorical question are welcome; the tone is that of a knowledgeable friend in the press room, not a broadcaster reading to camera.

4. **Personality is expressed through sentence-level rhythm and word choice**, not through first-person declarations ("I think") — authorial confidence is structural, not self-referential.

5. **Address the subject (driver, team) directly in the third person with human texture**: drivers are named by surname on second reference; their emotional state, radio tone, or post-race demeanor is read as evidence, not gossip.

6. **Maintain a clear distinction between factual report and judgment**: reported events are in past tense; verdicts and implications shift to present or future tense.

7. **The register permits mild sarcasm and understatement for emphasis** ("that's … something?"; "nice start"; "not exactly a 'come and get me'"), used sparingly to land a point without undermining analytical credibility.

8. **Avoid cliché F1 broadcast language** (e.g., "lights to flag," "on the limit throughout"); favor fresh, concrete phrasing over received idiom.

---

## PACING

1. **Individual entries are between 80 and 250 words** — short enough to read in one breath, long enough to contain a real argument; the range varies with the complexity of the storyline, not with the driver's championship standing.

2. **The opening claim sentence of each entry is almost always the shortest sentence in that entry**: a punchy declarative that earns the reader's attention before the elaboration begins.

3. **Density peaks in the middle of each entry**, where quote integration, numerical evidence, and mechanical/strategic explanation accumulate; the entry then releases tension with a shorter concluding sentence.

4. **Across the full piece, pace is managed by alternating entry length**: a short, sharp entry (80–120 words) follows a longer, more analytically demanding one (180–250 words) to maintain reading momentum.

5. **Thematic "Everything we learned" entries run longer per section** (200–400 words) because they carry explanatory rather than evaluative weight; within those sections, paragraph breaks are used every 3–5 sentences to prevent density from becoming oppressive.

6. **Quotations are integrated mid-entry rather than used as openers or closers**, preventing the structural dependence on external voices; a quote earns its place when it adds texture or precision that paraphrase cannot.

7. **The piece as a whole has no formal conclusion**: the final entry ends with the same bylined kicker structure as all others — there is no summative paragraph, no recap, no sign-off. The piece simply stops when the significant stories are exhausted.

8. **Intra-entry transitions are syntactic, not connective** — "But," "And," "Yet," "So" begin sentences freely rather than being relegated to clause-linking duty; this keeps rhythm brisk without sacrificing logical flow.
